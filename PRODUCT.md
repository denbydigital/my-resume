# Product

## Register

brand

## Users

Two audiences, both evaluating the same question from different angles:

- **Hiring managers and technical recruiters** at software companies — screening candidates for senior IC or leadership roles. They want to quickly gauge seniority, depth, and whether Laura fits the culture.
- **Startup founders and CTOs** considering a consultant or fractional hire. They need to trust that Laura can deliver, not just advise. They're buying judgment, not hours.

Both arrive with some skepticism and leave when something feels off. The site needs to earn trust in the first scroll and sustain it through every detail.

## Product Purpose

Laura Denby's personal site is the primary artifact that represents her professionally. It replaces a PDF resume for most high-value conversations, and sets the tone before any interview or client call. Success means the reader finishes the site thinking "I want to talk to this person" — not "impressive credentials."

## Brand Personality

Sharp, warm, credible. The combination that's genuinely rare: technically rigorous and easy to work with. Not a faceless expert, not a people-pleaser — someone who's done hard things and can talk about them plainly.

## Anti-references

- Generic "senior engineer portfolio" sites: dark background, syntax-highlighted hero, GitHub streak graph as a credential.
- Agency-style consultant pages with transformation language, ROI claims, and testimonial carousels.
- Cream/sand/linen backgrounds used as a default "warmth" move — warmth should come from voice and color, not a warm-tinted near-white body.
- Eyebrow labels on every section as scaffolding reflex.

## Design Principles

1. **Earn trust, don't claim it.** Show the work and the thinking; let the quality speak. No self-promotional superlatives.
2. **One audience at a time.** Every section should have a clear primary reader. Write for them; the other audience will follow.
3. **Restraint as signal.** A calm, unhurried page communicates confidence. Busyness communicates anxiety.
4. **Details compound.** The micro-interactions, the typographic rhythm, the word choice in a button label — each one either reinforces or undercuts the overall impression.
5. **The color does emotional work.** Don't leave it neutral. Pick a direction that has a point of view.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG AA as a baseline — 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text. Reduced-motion alternatives for all animations. Keyboard navigable throughout.
